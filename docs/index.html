Abstract

This page introduces quasiperiodic functions in a visual and accessible way. A quasiperiodic pattern follows a clear rule, but it does not repeat exactly like a normal periodic pattern. The examples below start with simple waves, then move to geodesics on tori, quasicrystal patterns, and level curves connected to Novikov's problem. The interactive tools are included so readers can change parameters and see how the mathematics behaves.

1. Introduction

1.1 What Are Quasiperiodic Functions?

A periodic pattern repeats after a fixed distance or time. A quasiperiodic pattern is different: it has structure, but it does not repeat exactly. One simple way to create this behavior is to combine two waves whose frequencies do not fit into a simple ratio.

Everyday Example: Moiré Patterns

When two regular grids overlap at slightly different angles, they create a larger pattern called a moire pattern. The result can look organized without repeating in a simple way. This is a helpful first picture of quasiperiodicity.

1.2 The Discovery of Quasicrystals

For a long time, crystallography allowed only certain rotational symmetries. Four-fold and six-fold symmetries were common, but five-fold symmetry was considered incompatible with periodic crystals.

In 1982, Dan Shechtman observed an aluminum-manganese alloy with sharp diffraction spots and five-fold symmetry. The pattern was ordered, but it was not periodic in the usual crystal sense.

The discovery was controversial at first, but it was later confirmed. Shechtman received the 2011 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for discovering quasicrystals.

1.3 Applications

Quasiperiodic structures have practical applications:

  • Coatings: Quasicrystalline coatings provide hardness and low friction
  • Photonics: Photonic quasicrystals control light propagation in ways periodic structures cannot
  • Materials: Quasicrystalline alloys have unique mechanical properties
  • Research: Quasiperiodic structures are studied for potential applications in quantum systems

2. Mathematical Foundations

2.1 Starting Simple: What Does "Periodic" Mean?

A function is periodic if it repeats exactly after some fixed interval.

Definition (Periodic Function - Simple Version)
A function repeats with period $T$ if shifting the input by $T$ gives the same output: $$f(x + T) = f(x) \text{ for all } x$$

Example: Sine Wave

The function $\sin(x)$ repeats every $2\pi$:

  • $\sin(0) = 0$
  • $\sin(2\pi) = 0$
  • $\sin(4\pi) = 0$

In general, $\sin(x + 2\pi) = \sin(x)$ for any $x$.

2.2 The Leap to Quasiperiodicity

Now here's the key insight: what if we add two periodic functions with incommensurable periods (periods whose ratio is irrational)?

Worked Example: Building a Quasiperiodic Function

Consider two cosine waves:

  • Wave 1: $\cos(x)$ with period $2\pi$
  • Wave 2: $\cos(\sqrt{2}x)$ with period $2\pi/\sqrt{2}$

The combined function is: $f(x) = \cos(x) + \cos(\sqrt{2}x)$

Question: Does this combined function repeat?

Answer: No. Because $\sqrt{2}$ is irrational, the two waves do not share a common period. The combined pattern keeps changing without repeating exactly.

2.3 The Torus: A Perfect Home for Quasiperiodicity

The torus is a useful way to understand repeating and non-repeating motion. It can be pictured either as a doughnut-shaped surface or as a square whose opposite edges are connected.

Building a Torus

  1. Start with a square: Take a square piece of paper
  2. Make a cylinder: Roll it up and glue the top edge to the bottom edge
  3. Make a torus: Bend the cylinder and glue the two circular ends together

Alternatively, we can think of the torus as a square where:

  • When you walk off the right edge, you appear on the left
  • When you walk off the top edge, you appear on the bottom

This is similar to the wraparound behavior in classic video games.

2.4 Geodesics: Straight Lines on a Torus

If you walk in a straight line on a torus (without turning), your path is called a geodesic. The behavior depends on the slope:

Mathematical Properties

When you draw a line with slope $m = \Delta y/\Delta x$ on the flat torus:

  • If $m$ is rational (like $2/3$), the geodesic closes after going around 2 times vertically and 3 times horizontally
  • If $m$ is irrational (like $\sqrt{2}$), the geodesic never closes but comes arbitrarily close to every point. This is called being dense

2.5 Connecting Geodesics to Quasiperiodic Functions

The connection is direct: following a straight path on a torus can produce the same kind of non-repeating signal as a quasiperiodic function.

Connection to Quasiperiodic Functions

Consider a function $F(u, v)$ defined on the torus (where $u$ and $v$ are angles from 0 to $2\pi$). Walking along a straight line with slope $\omega$, the position at time $t$ is:

  • $u(t) = t \pmod{2\pi}$
  • $v(t) = \omega t \pmod{2\pi}$

The function traced out is: $f(t) = F(t, \omega t)$

When $\omega$ is irrational, this produces a quasiperiodic function.

3. Quasiperiodic Functions in Multiple Variables

3.1 From Lines to Surfaces

So far, the examples used one input variable. The same ideas also work for functions of two or more variables, where patterns can spread across a plane or through space.

Definition (Quasiperiodic Function in Multiple Variables)
A function $f(x, y)$ is quasiperiodic if we can write it as: $$f(x, y) = F(\omega_1 x + \omega_2 y, \omega_3 x + \omega_4 y)$$ where $F$ is periodic in both arguments, and the frequencies $\omega_1, \omega_2, \omega_3, \omega_4$ are chosen so the function never exactly repeats.

3.2 A Concrete Example: Quasiperiodic Wallpaper

Example: 2D Quasiperiodic Pattern

For example, this function creates a quasiperiodic pattern in the plane:

$$f(x, y) = \cos(x + y) + \cos(\sqrt{2}x - y) + \cos(x + \phi y)$$

where $\phi = (1 + \sqrt{5})/2$ is the golden ratio.

This function combines three wave patterns:

  1. One moving diagonally at 45°
  2. One at an irrational angle involving $\sqrt{2}$
  3. One at an angle involving the golden ratio

The result is a pattern with visible structure but no exact repeating tile.

3.4 Understanding Winding Numbers

Winding Numbers Explained

On a 3D torus (doughnut shape), a geodesic can wind around in two ways:

  • Poloidal winding: Around the "small circle" (through the doughnut hole)
  • Toroidal winding: Around the "big circle" (around the doughnut's body)

If the winding numbers form a rational ratio, the geodesic closes. If the ratio is irrational, the path does not close and becomes dense on the surface.

4. Connections to Quasicrystals

4.1 From Mathematics to Matter

Quasicrystals show how this mathematics appears in real materials. They are ordered like crystals, but their order is not periodic.

The Atomic Jigsaw Puzzle

A traditional crystal repeats like a regular floor tile pattern. A quasicrystal is ordered, but it does not repeat by simple translation. Penrose tilings give a useful two-dimensional model of this idea.

4.2 The Cut-and-Project Method

One method for constructing quasicrystals uses higher-dimensional geometry:

The Cut-and-Project Method

  1. Start with a higher-dimensional lattice (e.g., a 6D cubic lattice)
  2. Choose a lower-dimensional subspace at an irrational angle
  3. Project nearby lattice points onto this subspace
  4. Result: A quasiperiodic structure

Taking a slice through a regular grid at an irrational angle produces intersection points that never repeat periodically.

5.3 Level Set Explorer & Novikov's Problem

Tool 3: Quasiperiodic Level Set Explorer

This tool draws level curves of the function:

$$f(x,y) = \cos(2\pi x) + \cos(2\pi y) + \cos(2\pi\alpha x)$$

The level value $c$ selects the points where $f(x,y)=c$. The app shows a finite, movable window in the plane. Blue curves close inside the current view. Orange curves touch the edge, which means the visible piece continues outside the window.

Novikov's problem and physical motivation

Novikov's problem studies whether level curves of quasiperiodic functions close into loops or continue through the plane. Related questions appear in solid-state physics, where electron paths in metals can follow level sets of a Fermi surface under a magnetic field.

This page gives a visual introduction to that question. It does not prove global behavior from one finite window; it helps users explore the local shapes of the curves.

How to use: Adjust $\alpha$ to change the pattern, then move the level value $c$ to see how the curves split, merge, close, or leave the view.

5.2 Numerical Precision and Challenges

Computational limits

Computers can't store irrational numbers exactly, so we use approximations. This means:

  • A "quasiperiodic" geodesic might eventually close due to rounding
  • We limit animations to prevent infinite loops
  • Real quasiperiodicity is a mathematical idealization

Even with these limits, the visualizations show the main behavior clearly.

6. Special Functions and Examples

6.1 The Triply Periodic Function

Now let's explore a specific function that appears in many physical applications:

The Canonical Triply Periodic Function

$$F(x,y,z) = \cos(x)\cos(y) + \cos(y)\cos(z) + \cos(z)\cos(x)$$

6.2 Understanding Through Level Sets

To visualize this 3D function, we look at its level sets: surfaces where $F(x,y,z) = c$ for different constants $c$.

What You're Seeing

When $c = 0$, the level set $F(x,y,z) = 0$ creates the Schwarz P surface. This surface separates space into two interwoven regions.

Figure 5: Cross-sections of the triply periodic function at different heights.

6.3 From Periodic to Quasiperiodic

To make this function quasiperiodic, we evaluate it along an irrational direction:

Creating Quasiperiodicity

Instead of $F(x,y,z)$, consider:

$$f(t) = F(t, \sqrt{2}t, \phi t)$$

where $\phi$ is the golden ratio. This traces the 3D periodic structure along irrational directions, producing a one-dimensional quasiperiodic function.

6.4 Piecewise Linear Examples: μG4 and μG7

Not all interesting functions are smooth. The μG4 and μG7 families are piecewise linear functions that arise from polyhedra.

What Are μG4 and μG7?

Imagine creating a surface from flat polygonal faces (like origami). The height function above any point gives a piecewise linear function. When we take irrational slices through these polyhedral surfaces, we get quasiperiodic piecewise linear functions.

μG4: Based on a 4-connected graph (each vertex connects to 4 edges)

μG7: Based on a 7-connected graph (each vertex connects to 7 edges)

These appear in:

  • Crystallography (atomic surface models)
  • Materials science (grain boundaries)
  • Mathematical physics (integrable systems)

7. Physical Applications

7.1 Electronic Properties of Quasiperiodic Systems

When electrons move through a quasiperiodic potential, as in a quasicrystal, their behavior can differ from what happens in ordinary periodic crystals.

Electronic behavior

In periodic crystals, electrons form energy bands. In quasiperiodic systems:

  • The spectrum can be a fractal (Cantor set)
  • Electronic states can be "critical": neither extended nor localized
  • Conductivity shows unusual temperature dependence

7.2 Magnetoresistance of Lead

Lead provides a fascinating example of quasiperiodicity in physics. When you apply a magnetic field to lead, its electrical resistance oscillates in a complex pattern.

The Lead Story

What happens: In a magnetic field, electrons in lead follow helical paths. The resistance depends on how these paths interact with the Fermi surface (the surface in momentum space separating occupied from unoccupied electron states).

The quasiperiodic connection: The Fermi surface of lead has a complex shape. When electron orbits sample this surface at different magnetic fields, they create quasiperiodic oscillations in resistance.

Why it matters: These oscillations help reveal the shape of the Fermi surface, which is important for understanding how electrons move in the material.

Simplified Model

The magnetoresistance oscillations can be approximated as:

$$\Delta R(B) \sim \sum_i A_i \cos\left(\frac{2\pi F_i}{B} + \phi_i\right)$$

where:

  • $B$ is the magnetic field strength
  • $F_i$ are characteristic frequencies related to extremal orbits on the Fermi surface
  • When ratios $F_i/F_j$ are irrational, the pattern is quasiperiodic.

7.3 Modern Applications

Where quasiperiodicity appears

Photonic Quasicrystals

  • Control light propagation in novel ways
  • Create optical devices with unique properties
  • Enable better solar cells and LEDs

Metamaterials

  • Quasiperiodic arrangements of resonators
  • Unusual acoustic or electromagnetic properties
  • Potential for cloaking devices

Twisted Bilayer Graphene

  • Two graphene layers at an irrational angle
  • Creates quasiperiodic moiré patterns
  • Exhibits superconductivity at "magic angles"

8. Summary and Future Directions

8.1 Summary

Key properties of quasiperiodic functions:

  1. Deterministic but non-repeating: They follow precise rules yet never repeat exactly
  2. Interdisciplinary: They connect pure mathematics, crystallography, and materials science
  3. Non-standard symmetries: They can exhibit symmetries forbidden in periodic structures
  4. Practical applications: Used in coatings, photonics, and materials engineering
